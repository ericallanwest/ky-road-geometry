Where friction matters

Road geometry from public lidar along 2,060 miles of eastern Kentucky's state routes, checked against the state's own inventories and turned into a map of how much grip the road asks of a car.

How much friction a road needs depends on its geometry: how sharp its curves are, how steep its grades, how much its lanes bank, and how far ahead a driver can see. Kentucky publishes the ingredients: the Kentucky Transportation Cabinet's road network with its curve, grade, speed-limit and bridge inventories, and KyFromAbove's statewide lidar, a bare-earth elevation model at 2-ft resolution.

This project derives the geometry from those sources for KYTC's District 12, seven mountain counties in the eastern coalfield, and checks it against the inventories. It then combines them into friction demand: the share of a car's weight its tires must deliver, braking and cornering, at the speed the road is likely driven. It measures demand only. What the pavement supplies, and what level of demand calls for a closer look, are other questions.

How it works

  1. Network. KYTC's All Roads layer for the seven counties, US and KY routes, with each feature's measures anchored to its milepoints. Divided highways' second carriageways, the -010 routes, are turned to run with the mainline's measure.
  2. Lidar, read once. KyFromAbove's 2-ft tiles are cloud-optimized GeoTIFFs on AWS, about 30 MB each. Only the 512-by-512 blocks within 65 ft of a road are read, and the corridor is kept locally: 26,771 blocks, the newest lidar phase first. Which tile is read where comes from a dated snapshot of the tile indexes.
  3. Profile and grade. Elevation every 0.005 mi along each route, by bilinear interpolation. Grade is the central difference over 52.8 ft. A bare-earth model has the bridges taken out, so dips where the profile falls steeply into a valley and climbs back are found and decked over, along with every recorded bridge.
  4. Curves. Curvature from 50-ft chords of the route line. A curve is a run of one direction above a 3,048-ft radius. Its radius comes from the curvature of its core, the part at 80% or more of its peak, so spirals don't stretch it. A circle fit to the core flags curves that are really two.
  5. Cross slope. The lidar across the right-hand lane, 4 and 8 ft out from the route line, each averaged along 24 ft. Reading within one lane keeps the crown from cancelling itself. On a curve the lane's bank is its superelevation.
  6. Sight distance. From a 3.5-ft eye to a 2-ft object, the farthest point a crest leaves in view, and at night in a sag, where a 2-ft headlight beam aimed 1° up meets the road. Both are computed on the profile itself, each way. Try the same code on a curve of your own in the crest and sag explorer.
  7. Speed. The posted limit plus 5 mph, capped on curves at √(gR(e+f)) with AASHTO's side friction, then ramped: braking at 5 ft/s² into a curve, accelerating at 3 ft/s² out of it.
  8. Demand. Along the road, |a/g + G/100|; across it, |V²/gR − e/100|; combined on the friction circle. Separately, the friction it takes to stop within the sight distance a crest or sag leaves, after 2.5 s to react.
  9. Crashes. Each police report is placed on the route it names, in its county, at the grid point nearest its coordinates. Rates use each KYTC traffic section's latest count, halved on each carriageway of a divided highway; the rate ratios come from a negative binomial model of each 0.1-mile piece's crashes on its demand and log traffic, with its vehicle-miles as exposure.

Data and code

Road network and inventories: Kentucky Transportation Cabinet, public ArcGIS feature services (KYTC), fetched 2026-10-30. Elevation: KyFromAbove bare-earth DEMs on the AWS Open Data registry, tile indexes snapshotted 2026-10-30. Ruggedness and rurality: USDA Economic Research Service, Area and Road Ruggedness Scales (2020 tracts, with each tract's primary RUCA code); tract boundaries: Census Bureau TIGER/Line 2020. Crashes: Kentucky State Police reports from the public search at crashinformationky.org (© KSP, all rights reserved), counted here, not republished. This project isn't affiliated with KYTC, KyFromAbove or KSP.

Code: github.com/ericallanwest/ky-road-geometry (Python: NumPy, pandas, Shapely, rasterio, pyproj; MapLibre on this page).
